Execution, from order to fill

Order types, smart order routing and liquidity aggregation, the latency budget, pre-trade risk checks, and how to measure what execution actually cost.

  • execution
  • latency
  • market data
  • hedging
  • risk systems
  • measurement

"Buy 1,000" is an instruction. What the client receives is a set of fills at prices, times and venues, and the difference between the two is execution. Most of the work happens in the few milliseconds nobody sees, and the quality of it only becomes visible in aggregate — which is why it is measured badly so often.

Order types are risk choices

Every order type trades certainty of execution against certainty of price.

OrderGuaranteesRisks
MarketIt fills (if there is liquidity)The price, in a thin or fast market
LimitThe worst priceThat it never fills
StopThat it triggers at a levelThe fill price after triggering — a stop becomes a market order
Stop-limitTrigger and worst priceTriggering, then not filling, while the market runs away
IOC / FOKNo resting order left behindPartial or no fill

The useful framing for clients and for product teams alike: a market order buys certainty that you trade and pays for it in price; a limit order buys certainty of price and pays for it in the risk of missing the trade. A stop is not protection against gaps — when the market opens through it, the fill is wherever the market is.

Smart order routing

When the same instrument trades in several places, an order has to decide where to go. A smart order router takes the parent order and the current state of each venue and produces child orders:

  1. Build the consolidated book from each venue's depth.
  2. Adjust each venue's prices for its fees, rebates and expected fill probability — the headline price is not the price you get.
  3. Walk the book, taking the best effective prices until the order is filled or the limit is reached.
  4. Send the child orders, ideally so that they arrive together, because the first fill is a signal to anyone watching the other venues.
  5. Handle what comes back: partial fills, rejects, and quotes that had moved by the time the order arrived.

Step 4 is where latency stops being an engineering metric and becomes a cost. Orders that arrive at different venues a few milliseconds apart give faster participants time to adjust the quotes the router was relying on.

Liquidity aggregation

A broker or dealer that hedges through several liquidity providers runs the same logic in a different setting. Each provider streams quotes, often with different sizes, different validity, and different rules about whether a request at that price will actually be honoured. Last look — a provider's right to reject a trade request within a short window after seeing it — means a quote is an offer to consider, not a commitment.

The aggregator therefore keeps statistics, not just prices: per provider, the fill ratio, the reject ratio by market condition, how long the last look takes, and the markouts after the fill. A provider with the best headline price and a high reject rate in fast markets is often the most expensive one in the book.

At Revolut the liquidity aggregation, smart order routing and hedging algorithms ran with a roundtrip under 60 milliseconds. The number matters less than the discipline behind it: knowing where each millisecond went.

The latency budget

End-to-end latency is a sum, and each term has an owner:

  • market data in: venue → network → feed handler → book;
  • decision: risk checks, pricing, routing logic;
  • order out: gateway → network → venue;
  • acknowledgement and fill back, through the same path.

Measuring only the total tells you there is a problem and nothing about where. Timestamping at each boundary — and keeping the clocks that write those timestamps in agreement — turns "the platform is slow" into "the risk check added 4 milliseconds after the last release", which is a problem someone can fix.

Tail latency matters more than the average. A system that answers in 2 milliseconds at the median and 400 at the 99.9th percentile will hurt clients precisely on the busy days, because the tail is where the volume is.

Pre-trade risk checks

Every order passes through checks before it leaves: does the client have the margin, is the size inside limits, is the price within a band of the market, is this order a duplicate, is the instrument tradable right now. They are the cheapest insurance a platform buys, and they sit directly in the latency path, so two properties matter:

  • They must be fast and deterministic — no network calls on the hot path that can stall.
  • They must fail closed. If a check cannot decide, the order does not go. A risk check that times out and lets the order through is not a risk check.

Fat-finger price bands deserve particular care: too tight and they reject legitimate orders on volatile days, too loose and they let through the error they exist to stop.

Measuring what it cost

Execution quality is measured against a benchmark, and the choice of benchmark decides the answer.

  • Arrival price. The mid when the order was decided. The gap to the average fill price is implementation shortfall: everything execution cost, including the market moving while you worked the order.
  • Spread capture. Where inside the bid-offer the fill landed.
  • VWAP. Fill price against the volume-weighted average over the period. Useful for large orders worked over time; flattering for small ones, which are part of the volume they are measured against.
  • Markouts. The same post-trade measure a market maker uses, from the client's side: did the price keep moving after the fill?

Regulation in Europe frames all of this as best execution: firms must take all sufficient steps to obtain the best possible result for clients, considering price, costs, speed, likelihood of execution and settlement, size and nature. The practical consequence is that every one of the numbers above has to be computable after the fact, per order, with the market data that was true at the time — which takes us back to market data.

The short version

  • Pick order types for the risk you would rather carry.
  • Route on effective prices, not headline ones.
  • Score liquidity providers on fills and markouts, not quotes.
  • Budget latency per hop, and watch the tail.
  • Keep pre-trade checks fast, deterministic and closed on failure.
  • Measure against arrival price before anything that flatters you.
