A price on a screen looks like a fact about now. It is a claim about the past,
made by a venue, carried through several systems that each changed it a little,
and displayed some milliseconds — or seconds — after the event it describes.
Every system that prices, margins or executes against that number inherits
those changes, whether or not anyone wrote them down.

Market data is the least glamorous part of a trading platform and the one most
likely to be quietly wrong. Pricing models get reviewed. Feed handlers mostly
get noticed when they stop.

## What actually arrives

Venues publish at different depths, and the depth decides what you can know.

| Level | What it carries | What it lets you answer |
|---|---|---|
| L1 | Best bid, best offer, last trade | Where is the market? |
| L2 | Aggregated size at each price level | How much can I trade before the price moves? |
| L3 | Every individual order, add / modify / cancel | Who is queued ahead of me, and is that size real? |

Most retail-facing systems run on L1 and a few levels of L2. That is enough to
show a price, and not enough to know what a large order will cost — which is
why execution systems and display systems often subscribe to the same
instrument at different depths.

The other distinction that matters is **snapshot versus incremental**. A
snapshot is the whole book; an incremental feed is a stream of changes to it.
Incremental feeds are smaller and faster, and they carry a sequence number for
a reason: apply one update out of order, or miss one, and every book you build
afterwards is wrong in a way that looks perfectly plausible.

## Normalisation is where meaning changes

Each venue has its own symbology, tick sizes, lot sizes, price multipliers,
trading calendars and conventions for what a "trade" is. A feed handler
translates all of that into one internal model, and every translation is a
decision someone made once and nobody revisits:

- **Instrument identity.** The same economic thing can be several listings,
  and one ticker can mean different things on different venues. Mapping errors
  here do not crash anything; they price one instrument off another.
- **Price scaling.** Pence versus pounds, quoted versus clean price, a futures
  multiplier applied twice or not at all. A factor of 100 is easy to spot. A
  factor of 1.0125 is not.
- **Trade conditions.** Auction prints, off-book trades, late reports and
  corrections all arrive as "trades". A last-price field that includes them is
  measuring something different from one that does not.
- **Calendars.** Half-days, holidays and session breaks decide whether a quiet
  feed is a closed market or a broken connection.

The useful habit is to keep the raw message alongside the normalised one for
long enough to answer "what did the venue actually say?" When a client disputes
a fill, that question arrives within the hour.

## Gaps, recovery and the book you cannot trust

Sequence numbers make gaps detectable; they do not make them harmless. When a
gap appears the honest state of the book is *unknown*, and the system has to
choose:

1. **Stop and recover.** Mark the instrument stale, request a snapshot or
   replay, rebuild, then resume. Correct, and briefly blind.
2. **Carry on.** Keep applying updates to a book known to be missing some.
   Fast, and wrong in a way that cannot be measured.

Anything that prices or margins should take the first option and say so. The
second is how a crossed book — best bid above best offer — ends up feeding a
risk engine, which then reports a profit that does not exist.

Crossed and locked markets are worth checking for explicitly. They are rare on
a single healthy venue, common across venues for a few milliseconds, and almost
always a sign of a stale side when they persist.

## Conflation and staleness

A busy instrument can update tens of thousands of times a second, and most
consumers cannot use that. **Conflation** keeps the latest state and drops the
intermediate ones: a screen redrawing at 4Hz needs the price as of now, not
every price since the last frame.

Conflation is right for display and dangerous for anything that needs the
path — a stop that should have triggered at a price the conflated stream never
showed, or a volume-weighted calculation computed over a sample instead of the
trades.

Staleness is the mirror problem: a price that has not changed may be a quiet
market or a dead connection. The two look identical in the data. Good systems
carry the time of the last update next to every price, and the age of that
timestamp — not the price — is what a dealer looks at first when something
seems off. A coloured "seconds since last update" next to a figure is one of
the most useful pieces of UI in a dealing tool, because it answers the question
before it is asked.

## Timestamps, plural

A single tick can carry several times:

- when the venue matched or published it;
- when it reached your network;
- when your handler decoded it;
- when your application acted on it.

The gaps between them are your latency budget, measured. Keeping only the last
one — usually the local receive time — throws away the ability to say whether
a slow fill was the venue, the network or your own code. Clocks also need to
agree: a timestamp from a server that has drifted by a few milliseconds will
make causality appear to run backwards in your logs.

## What a composite price hides

Platforms that quote one price per instrument usually build it from several
sources: venues, liquidity providers, a reference feed. The composite is a
policy, not a measurement. It has to decide which sources count, how to weight
them, how to treat one that has gone quiet, and what to do when they disagree.

That policy is invisible in the output. A smooth composite can hide one source
that has been stale for a minute and another that has started printing outliers.
The defence is to keep each source's contribution inspectable — which source
set the bid, how old it was, how far the others were from it — so that when the
composite moves, someone can say why.

## The short version

- Treat every price as *price, source, time* — never price alone.
- Prefer a stale flag to a confident wrong number.
- Recover from gaps; do not paper over them.
- Conflate for people, not for risk.
- Keep the raw message long enough to answer a dispute.
