Watch these for the culture, not the mechanics — all of them compress and
simplify, and some of them are fiction throughout. Ranked roughly by how much
of the job they get right.

## The ones that get it right

**Margin Call** (2011). The best of them: a risk model returns an unacceptable
number overnight, and the rest is people deciding what to do about it.
Understated, dialogue-driven, and accurate about hierarchy in a way the others
are not. If you watch one, watch this.

**The Big Short** (2015). The clearest popular explanation of CDOs and credit
default swaps ever put on screen, at the cost of some caricature. Conceptually
sound, and the fourth-wall explainers earn their place. Read the book for the
mechanics.

**Trading Places** (1983). A comedy that ends in a commodities pit, and
genuinely useful on how a corner works. Also responsible, indirectly, for a
rule in US law about trading on misappropriated government data.

## The ones about the culture

**Wall Street** (1987) and **Money Never Sleeps** (2010). Dated, and still the
cultural template for the whole era — "greed is good" came from here, and was
not meant as advice. Watch the first; the sequel is for completeness.

**Boiler Room** (2000). Pump-and-dump brokerage culture. More about sales cults
than about trading, which is precisely what makes it useful: the pressure it
depicts is real and has not gone anywhere.

**The Wolf of Wall Street** (2013). Stratton Oakmont at full volume. About
excess and fraud rather than markets. Enjoyable, frequently mistaken for
aspirational, and worth watching only if you keep that straight.
